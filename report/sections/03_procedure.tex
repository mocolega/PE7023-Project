\subsection{Well Design}
This subsecion describes the design of the default well. Because the wells were not drilled, it is necessary to specify the tubing and production casing.
Is was informed that the inflow can be modeled as a single-phase steady-state solution of the Darcy equation for a vertical fully penetrating well.
The Productivity Index (PI) equation is given by:
\begin{equation}
    PI = \frac{q_{liq}}{\bar{p} - p_{wf}} = \frac{kh}{141.2 B_o \mu_o \left[\ln{\left(\frac{r_e}{r_{wf}}\right)}-0.5 + S\right]} \quad \text{STB/d/psi}
\end{equation}
Where $q_{liq}$ is the liquid flow rate in STB/d, $\bar{p}$ is the average reservoir pressure, $p_{wf}$ is the bottom hole flowing pressure (psi), $k$ is the permeability in mD, $h$ is the thickness of the reservoir in ft, $B_o$ is the oil formation volume factor (RB/STB), $\mu_o$ is the oil viscosity (cP), $r_e$ is the drainage radius (ft), $r_{wf}$ is the wellbore radius (ft) and $S$ is the skin factor.
The reservoir properties are given in Table \ref{tab:reservoir_properties}.

\subsubsection{Boundary Conditions}

To model the subsea pipeline independently from the well network, the pipeline
was treated as a mass-to-pressure boundary condition system. The inlet
conditions at manifold~2 (m2) were derived from the well-level PIPESIM
simulations carried out in Part~1.1 (Question~I-6), assuming all seven wells
behave identically to W1. Because gas-lift injection gas flows through the
pipeline, the effective pipeline GOR was computed as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{GOR}_{\text{pipeline}} = \text{GOR}_{\text{sat}} +
    \frac{G_{\text{inj}}}{q_{o,\text{pipeline}}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Diameter Selection}

the combined well output at m2, a single flowline following the 14-point

\subsection*{Optimization of Operating Conditions}

It is possible to optimize the operating conditions for each water cut scenario to maximize the net profit. The net profit is given by the difference between the revenue and the costs. The revenue is given by the product of the oil sale price and the oil production, while the costs are given by the sum of the water treatment cost and the gas-lift injection cost.
The optimization process was made using a simple algorithm that evaluates the net profit for different gas-lift rates. This way, the well will produce at a flow rate less than the maximum allowed, but it will be more profitable. 
The implemented algorithm is shown in the following pseudocode. This process was applied for the two oil price scenarios and the same water treatment and gas-lift injection costs used in the previous sensitivity analysis. 

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Optimization of Operating Conditions}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
% \State Initialize water cut scenarios and corresponding production profiles
\For{each day in the production forecast}
    \State Get current water cut and corresponding production profile
    \State Initialize variables to store optimal gas-lift rate and maximum net profit
    \For{each gas-lift rate in the defined range}
        \State Calculate oil production based on current water cut and gas-lift rate
        \State Calculate revenue = oil sale price $\times$ oil production
        \State Calculate costs = water treatment cost + gas-lift injection cost
        \State Calculate net profit = revenue - costs
        \If{net profit > maximum net profit}
            \State Update maximum net profit and optimal gas-lift rate
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State Store optimal gas-lift rate for current day
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Part 2}
\label{sec:proc_phase2}


In this section will be discused the implementation of network simulator and the results obtained from the simulations.
The code was implemented in Python using Claude.ai as conding assistant. It was decided to make the subsurface and surface network in
a integrated way, so the network simulator will be able to evaluate the impact of the surface network on the subsurface and vice versa.

\section{Code Structure and Module Interaction}

The network simulator is organized as a set of modular Python files, where each file has a well-defined responsibility in the workflow. The code is divided into six main layers: input handling, fluid-property evaluation, multiphase pressure-drop models, physical network components, network solution, and visualization. This structure makes the simulator easier to validate, extend, and maintain.

\subsection{Overall Organization}

The simulator is centered on the idea that each physical object in the production system is represented explicitly in code. Fluid properties are evaluated first, then attached to physical components such as wells and flowlines. These components are assembled into a network graph, and the solver iterates on rates and pressures until the boundary conditions are satisfied. Finally, the results are post-processed and visualized.

\subsection*{\texttt{fluid\_correlations.py}}

This module provides the fluid-property model used throughout the simulator. Its main class is \texttt{BlackOilFluid}, which stores the fluid description and computes all required black-oil PVT and transport properties. In particular, it includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item solution gas--oil ratio, bubble-point pressure, and oil formation volume factor;
    \item oil, gas, and water densities and viscosities;
    \item gas compressibility factor using the Hall--Yarborough correlation;
    \item in-situ liquid and gas volumetric rates needed by the flow correlations.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, this module acts as the thermodynamic and transport-property foundation for all pressure-gradient calculations.

\subsection*{\texttt{multiphase\_correlations.py}}

This module contains the pressure-gradient models used by the simulator. From the calls in the rest of the code, its role is to provide:

\begin{itemize}
    \item vertical-flow calculations through the Hagedorn--Brown correlation;
    \item inclined-flow calculations through the Beggs--Brill correlation;
    \item a dispatcher function, \texttt{get\_correlation(...)}, that chooses the appropriate correlation based on inclination angle, pipe diameter, fluid object, and roughness.
\end{itemize}

In the present architecture, this file is the hydraulic core of the simulator. It converts local rate, pressure, and geometry information into pressure gradients.

\subsection*{\texttt{survey\_reader.py}}

This module is responsible for loading well trajectory data from external CSV files. It supports multiple survey formats, such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item measured depth and TVD only;
    \item measured depth and inclination;
    \item measured depth, TVD, and inclination together.
\end{itemize}

If one quantity is missing, the module reconstructs it using minimum-curvature-based inverse or forward calculations. The output is a list of \texttt{SurveyPoint} objects, which can be passed directly to the well model. This module isolates data ingestion from the rest of the simulator and makes the wellbore workflow independent of the original survey file format.

\subsection*{\texttt{network\_components.py}}

This module defines the physical components of the production network. It contains the objects that represent both the topology and the hydraulic elements of the system.

\subsubsection*{Node classes}

The code defines generic and specialized nodes such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{Node}: general network junction;
    \item \texttt{ReservoirNode}: fixed-pressure upstream boundary;
    \item \texttt{SeparatorNode}: fixed-pressure downstream sink;
    \item \texttt{FixedRateSink}: downstream sink constrained by total liquid rate;
    \item \texttt{ManifoldNode}: junction with no pressure drop.
\end{itemize}

These classes provide the boundary conditions and connection points for the network.

\subsubsection*{Well trajectory and well model}

The module also defines:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{SurveyPoint}: one survey station;
    \item \texttt{WellSegment}: one interval between survey stations;
    \item \texttt{Well}: the complete wellbore model.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{WellSegment} class computes the true vertical depth increment using the minimum-curvature method. The \texttt{Well} class then chains all segments together to construct the total measured depth and total TVD of the well.

In addition, the \texttt{Well} class contains the inflow model. It automatically switches between a linear productivity-index relation and a Vogel-type inflow relation, depending on reservoir pressure relative to bubble-point pressure. It can evaluate either:
\[
q = f(p_{wf})
\qquad \text{or} \qquad
p_{wf} = f(q),
\]
which allows the well to be coupled directly to the tubing-pressure calculations.

\subsubsection*{Pressure-drop elements}

For the outflow system, the module defines:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{FlowlinePipe}: a surface or subsea pipe represented by an elevation profile;
    \item \texttt{Choke}: a pressure-drop element using either a Gilbert-type or \(C_v\)-based model;
    \item \texttt{Manifold}: a zero-pressure-drop junction object.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{FlowlinePipe} object discretizes the pipe according to the supplied elevation profile and marches pressure from inlet to outlet. For each segment, it calls \texttt{get\_correlation(...)} to select the hydraulic correlation according to inclination. Similarly, the \texttt{Well} object marches pressure from bottom hole to wellhead, subdividing the survey into smaller intervals for improved accuracy.

\subsection*{\texttt{network\_solver.py}}

This module defines the global solver through the \texttt{ProductionNetwork} class. It is responsible for assembling all components into a connected directed tree and computing the operating state of the full network.

\subsubsection*{Graph construction}

The solver stores all nodes, wells, flowlines, chokes, and manifolds, then converts them into a graph representation with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item downstream connectivity;
    \item upstream connectivity;
    \item an edge map that links node pairs to the corresponding pipe or choke object.
\end{itemize}

This graph representation is used both for rate accumulation and for pressure marching.

\subsubsection*{Wellhead-performance logic}

A key feature of the solver is that it recognizes that the wellhead-pressure curve \(WHP(q)\) can be non-monotonic in multiphase flow. At low rate, the heavy liquid column causes a low wellhead pressure; at intermediate rate, gas lightens the column and the wellhead pressure rises; at still higher rate, friction dominates and the wellhead pressure decreases again. The solver therefore identifies the stable, descending branch of the \(WHP(q)\) curve and performs bisection only on that branch.

\subsubsection*{Boundary-condition handling}

The solver supports two main boundary-condition types:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fixed-pressure sink:} the separator pressure is prescribed, and the rates are obtained from the coupling between inflow and tubing/outflow performance.
    \item \textbf{Fixed-rate sink:} the total liquid rate is prescribed, and the solver determines the common wellhead pressure that makes the sum of the stable-branch well rates match the target throughput.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Network pressure march}

Once wellhead rates are known, the solver accumulates rates downstream through the graph and then marches pressures along flowlines and chokes. During this step, it also stores pressure-traverse profiles for later plotting.

\subsubsection*{Nodal analysis utility}

The same module also provides a helper function that generates IPR and OPR curves for a single well. This function is used later by the visualization layer to show the operating point in a classical nodal-analysis plot.

\subsection*{\texttt{plotting.py}}

This module contains the post-processing and visualization tools. Its functions create figures for the main engineering outputs of the simulator, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item nodal analysis curves (\texttt{plot\_nodal\_analysis});
    \item wellbore pressure profiles (\texttt{plot\_pressure\_profile});
    \item network node pressures and rates (\texttt{plot\_network\_pressures});
    \item flowline pressure traverses (\texttt{plot\_flowline\_profile});
    \item elevation profiles for pipelines (\texttt{plot\_pipeline\_elevation});
    \item a combined well dashboard (\texttt{plot\_well\_dashboard}).
\end{itemize}

These utilities are separate from the numerical core, which keeps the simulator structure clean and avoids mixing numerical algorithms with presentation logic.

\subsection*{\texttt{main\_wells.py}}

The main script acts as the execution layer of the simulator. Although its full code is not reproduced here, its role in the present architecture is to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item create fluid objects;
    \item read survey files;
    \item instantiate wells, flowlines, chokes, and boundary nodes;
    \item assemble them into a \texttt{ProductionNetwork};
    \item call the solver;
    \item request plots and organize the final outputs.
\end{itemize}

Thus, \texttt{main\_wells.py} is the entry point that connects the modules into a complete simulation workflow.

\subsection*{Summary of the Workflow}

The full workflow can be summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Survey data are read and converted into a usable trajectory representation.
    \item Fluid properties are evaluated from the black-oil model.
    \item Hydraulic correlations are selected according to inclination and flow conditions.
    \item Physical components such as wells, flowlines, chokes, and nodes are instantiated.
    \item The network solver applies the required boundary conditions and computes the rates and pressures across the system.
    \item The results are visualized through nodal plots, well profiles, flowline traverses, and network summary figures.
\end{enumerate}

This modular architecture allows each part of the simulator to be validated independently while preserving a clear integration path for full-field or multiwell network calculations.
