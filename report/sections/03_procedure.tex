\subsection{Well Design}
\label{subsec:well}
This subsection describes the design of the default well. Because the wells were
not drilled, it is necessary to specify the tubing and production casing.
It was informed that the inflow can be modeled as a single-phase steady-state
solution of the Darcy equation for a vertical fully penetrating well
\cite{lee_pressure_2003}. The Productivity Index (PI) equation is given by:
\begin{equation}
    PI = \frac{q_{liq}}{\bar{p} - p_{wf}} = \frac{kh}{141.2 B_o \mu_o
    \left[\ln{\left(\frac{r_e}{r_{wf}}\right)}-0.5 + S\right]}
    \quad \text{STB/d/psi}
\end{equation}
where $q_{liq}$ is the liquid flow rate in STB/d, $\bar{p}$ is the average
reservoir pressure, $p_{wf}$ is the bottomhole flowing pressure (psi), $k$ is
the permeability in mD, $h$ is the net pay thickness in ft, $B_o$ is the oil
formation volume factor (RB/STB), $\mu_o$ is the oil viscosity (cP), $r_e$ is
the drainage radius (ft), $r_{wf}$ is the wellbore radius (ft), and $S$ is the
skin factor \cite{lee_pressure_2003}.
The reservoir properties are given in Table~\ref{tab:reservoir_properties}.

To model the subsea pipeline independently from the well network, the pipeline
was treated as a mass-to-pressure boundary condition system \cite{Bai2005}. The
inlet conditions at manifold~2 (M2) were derived from the well-level
\emph{PIPESIM} \cite{Schlumberger2023} simulations carried out in Part~1
(Question~I-6), assuming all seven wells behave identically to W1. Because
gas-lift injection gas flows through the pipeline, the effective pipeline GOR
was computed as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{GOR}_{\text{pipeline}} = \text{GOR}_{\text{sat}} +
    \frac{G_{\text{inj}}}{q_{o,\text{pipeline}}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Diameter Selection}

A \emph{PIPESIM} \cite{Schlumberger2023} network model was constructed with a
source node representing the combined well output at M2, a single flowline
following the 14-point bathymetric survey of the M2--LOP corridor (total length
46{,}617~ft, maximum riser depth 3{,}000~ft), and a sink set at the separator
pressure of 350~psia. Black-oil fluid properties (API~45, GOR~800~scf/STB,
$\mu_o = 0.902$~cp, $B_o = 1.2$) were assigned \cite{Standing1977}. The
worst-case watercut scenario (WC~=~2\%) was used for diameter screening, as it
produced the highest inlet pressure for a given diameter.

wellhead pressure is maintained at or above 1{,}200~psia under all conditions,
a choke or back-pressure control valve could be installed downstream; however,
this would modify the effective pressure boundary condition and may require an
iterative update of the well-model boundary conditions.

Figure~\ref{fig:q4_temp} shows the corresponding outlet temperatures, which all
exceed the Wax Appearance Temperature (WAT) of 100\textdegree F for the 7-well

\subsection*{Optimization of Operating Conditions}

It is possible to optimize the operating conditions for each water cut scenario
to maximize the net profit. The net profit is given by the
difference between the revenue and the costs. The revenue is the product of the
oil sale price and the oil production, while the costs are the sum of the water
treatment cost and the gas-lift injection cost.
The optimization process used a simple algorithm that evaluates the net profit
for different gas-lift rates, implemented in Python. This
way, the well produces at a flow rate below the maximum allowed but at a more
profitable operating point. The implemented algorithm is shown in the following
pseudocode.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Optimization of Operating Conditions}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{each day in the production forecast}
    \State Get current water cut and corresponding production profile
    \State Initialize variables to store optimal gas-lift rate and maximum net profit
    \For{each gas-lift rate in the defined range}
        \State Calculate oil production based on current water cut and gas-lift rate
        \State Calculate revenue = oil sale price $\times$ oil production
        \State Calculate costs = water treatment cost + gas-lift injection cost
        \State Calculate net profit = revenue $-$ costs
        \If{net profit > maximum net profit}
            \State Update maximum net profit and optimal gas-lift rate
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State Store optimal gas-lift rate for current day
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Pipeline Operational Issues and Mitigation}
\label{subsubsec:pipeline_issues}

The 10-inch pipeline sized in Section~\ref{subsec:pipeline_design} performs
within constraints under the design conditions, but three operational issues
warrant attention over the field life.

\paragraph{Declining inlet pressure with increasing water cut.}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:q4_results}, the pipeline inlet pressure drops from
1{,}040~psia at WC~=~2\% to 785~psia at WC~=~95\%. Because the well network was
sized to maintain a minimum wellhead pressure of 1{,}200~psia, this falling
back-pressure at the manifold is beneficial for well deliverability. However, it
also means the pipeline operates increasingly below its design inlet pressure,
which changes the flow regime and mixture velocity over time \cite{BeggsBrill1973}.
A back-pressure control valve at the LOP can be used to regulate separator inlet
conditions and maintain stable flow.

\paragraph{Wax deposition risk under reduced production.}
At low watercut with only four wells online, the outlet temperature drops to
86.98\textdegree F, below the WAT of 100\textdegree F, with 1~inch of insulation.
This is the most critical flow assurance threat for this pipeline \cite{Bai2005}.
The selected mitigation is 2~inches of insulation, which raises the outlet
temperature to 107.5\textdegree F under worst-case conditions. During planned or
unplanned well shutdowns, particularly at early field life when water cut is low,
pigging should be scheduled regularly to remove any wax deposits that accumulate
during transient cool-down periods before the pipeline reaches steady-state
temperature.

\paragraph{Slugging at high gas-oil ratio.}
At high water cuts, the effective pipeline GOR increases substantially, from
800~scf/STB at WC~=~2\% to 5{,}778~scf/STB at WC~=~95\%, due to accumulating
gas-lift injection gas. The combination of a long flowline (46{,}617~ft), a
3{,}000~ft riser, and a high GOR creates conditions favorable for severe slugging
at the base of the riser \cite{BeggsBrill1973}. A slug catcher at the LOP
separator should be included in the facility design to handle the liquid surges
associated with slug arrival, and gas-lift injection rates should be managed to
avoid unnecessarily elevating the GOR beyond what is required for production
optimization.

\section{Part 2}
\label{sec:proc_phase2}

In this section the implementation of the network simulator and the results
obtained from the simulations are discussed. The code was implemented in Python
 using an AI coding assistant. The subsurface and surface
network were integrated so that the simulator evaluates the impact of the surface
network on the subsurface and vice versa.

\section{Code Structure and Module Interaction}

The network simulator is organized as a set of modular Python files
, where each file has a well-defined responsibility in the
workflow. The code is divided into six main layers: input handling,
fluid-property evaluation, multiphase pressure-drop models, physical network
components, network solution, and visualization. This structure makes the
simulator easier to validate, extend, and maintain.

\subsection{Overall Organization}

The simulator is centered on the idea that each physical object in the
production system is represented explicitly in code. Fluid properties are
evaluated first, then attached to physical components such as wells and
flowlines. These components are assembled into a network graph, and the solver
iterates on rates and pressures until the boundary conditions are satisfied.
Finally, the results are post-processed and visualized.

\subsection*{\texttt{fluid\_correlations.py}}

This module provides the fluid-property model used throughout the simulator.
Its main class is \texttt{BlackOilFluid}, which stores the fluid description
and computes all required black-oil PVT and transport properties
\cite{Standing1977}. In particular, it includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item solution gas-oil ratio, bubble-point pressure, and oil formation
          volume factor \cite{Standing1977};
    \item oil, gas, and water densities and viscosities;
    \item gas compressibility factor using the Hall-Yarborough correlation
          \cite{HallYarborough1974};
    \item in-situ liquid and gas volumetric rates needed by the flow
          correlations.
\end{itemize}

This module acts as the thermodynamic and transport-property foundation for all
pressure-gradient calculations.

\subsection*{\texttt{multiphase\_correlations.py}}

This module contains the pressure-gradient models used by the simulator
\cite{BeggsBrill1973,HagedornBrown1965}. Its role is to provide:

\begin{itemize}
    \item vertical-flow calculations through the Hagedorn-Brown correlation
          \cite{HagedornBrown1965};
    \item inclined-flow calculations through the Beggs-Brill correlation
          \cite{BeggsBrill1973};
    \item a dispatcher function, \texttt{get\_correlation(...)}, that chooses
          the appropriate correlation based on inclination angle.
\end{itemize}

In the present architecture, this file is the hydraulic core of the simulator.
It converts local rate, pressure, and geometry information into pressure
gradients.

\subsection*{\texttt{survey\_reader.py}}

This module is responsible for loading well trajectory data from external CSV
files. It supports multiple survey formats, such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item measured depth and TVD only;
    \item measured depth and inclination;
    \item measured depth, TVD, and inclination together.
\end{itemize}

If one quantity is missing, the module reconstructs it using
minimum-curvature-based inverse or forward calculations \cite{Wolff1981}. The
output is a list of \texttt{SurveyPoint} objects, which can be passed directly
to the well model. This module isolates data ingestion from the rest of the
simulator and makes the wellbore workflow independent of the original survey
file format.

\subsection*{\texttt{network\_components.py}}

This module defines the physical components of the production network. It
contains the objects that represent both the topology and the hydraulic elements
of the system.

\subsubsection*{Node classes}

The code defines generic and specialized nodes such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{Node}: general network junction;
    \item \texttt{ReservoirNode}: fixed-pressure upstream boundary;
    \item \texttt{SeparatorNode}: fixed-pressure downstream sink;
    \item \texttt{FixedRateSink}: downstream sink constrained by total liquid
          rate;
    \item \texttt{ManifoldNode}: junction with no pressure drop.
\end{itemize}

These classes provide the boundary conditions and connection points for the
network.

\subsubsection*{Well trajectory and well model}

The module also defines:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{SurveyPoint}: one survey station;
    \item \texttt{WellSegment}: one interval between survey stations;
    \item \texttt{Well}: the complete wellbore model.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{WellSegment} class computes the true vertical depth increment using
the minimum-curvature method \cite{Wolff1981}. The \texttt{Well} class then
chains all segments together to construct the total measured depth and total TVD
of the well.

In addition, the \texttt{Well} class contains the inflow model. It
automatically switches between a linear productivity-index relation
and a Vogel-type inflow relation \cite{Vogel1968}, depending
on reservoir pressure relative to bubble-point pressure. It can evaluate either:
\[
q = f(p_{wf})
\qquad \text{or} \qquad
p_{wf} = f(q),
\]
which allows the well to be coupled directly to the tubing-pressure
calculations.

\subsubsection*{Pressure-drop elements}

For the outflow system, the module defines:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{FlowlinePipe}: a surface or subsea pipe represented by an
          elevation profile;
    \item \texttt{Choke}: a pressure-drop element using either a Gilbert-type
          \cite{Gilbert1954} or $C_v$-based model;
    \item \texttt{Manifold}: a zero-pressure-drop junction object.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{FlowlinePipe} object discretizes the pipe according to the supplied
elevation profile and marches pressure from inlet to outlet. For each segment,
it calls \texttt{get\_correlation(...)} to select the hydraulic correlation
according to inclination \cite{BeggsBrill1973,HagedornBrown1965}. Similarly,
the \texttt{Well} object marches pressure from bottomhole to wellhead,
subdividing the survey into smaller intervals for improved accuracy.

\subsection*{\texttt{network\_solver.py}}

This module defines the global solver through the \texttt{ProductionNetwork}
class. It is responsible for assembling all components into a connected directed
tree and computing the operating state of the full network.

\subsubsection*{Graph construction}

The solver stores all nodes, wells, flowlines, chokes, and manifolds, then
converts them into a graph representation with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item downstream connectivity;
    \item upstream connectivity;
    \item an edge map that links node pairs to the corresponding pipe or choke
          object.
\end{itemize}

This graph representation is used both for rate accumulation and for pressure
marching.

\subsubsection*{Wellhead-performance logic}

A key feature of the solver is that it recognizes that the wellhead-pressure
curve $WHP(q)$ can be non-monotonic in multiphase flow \cite{BeggsBrill1973}.
At low rate, the heavy liquid column causes a low wellhead pressure; at
intermediate rate, gas lightens the column and the wellhead pressure rises; at
still higher rate, friction dominates and the wellhead pressure decreases again.
The solver therefore identifies the stable, descending branch of the $WHP(q)$
curve and performs bisection only on that branch.

\subsubsection*{Boundary-condition handling}

The solver supports two main boundary-condition types:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fixed-pressure sink:} the separator pressure is prescribed,
          and the rates are obtained from the coupling between inflow and
          tubing/outflow performance.
    \item \textbf{Fixed-rate sink:} the total liquid rate is prescribed, and
          the solver determines the common wellhead pressure that makes the sum
          of the stable-branch well rates match the target throughput.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Network pressure march}

Once wellhead rates are known, the solver accumulates rates downstream through
the graph and then marches pressures along flowlines and chokes. During this
step, it also stores pressure-traverse profiles for later plotting.

\subsubsection*{Nodal analysis utility}

The same module also provides a helper function that generates IPR and OPR
curves for a single well \cite{brown_nodal_1985}. This function is used later by the
visualization layer to show the operating point in a classical nodal-analysis
plot.

\subsection*{\texttt{plotting.py}}

This module contains the post-processing and visualization tools. Its functions
create figures for the main engineering outputs of the simulator, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item nodal analysis curves (\texttt{plot\_nodal\_analysis});
    \item wellbore pressure profiles (\texttt{plot\_pressure\_profile});
    \item network node pressures and rates (\texttt{plot\_network\_pressures});
    \item flowline pressure traverses (\texttt{plot\_flowline\_profile});
    \item elevation profiles for pipelines (\texttt{plot\_pipeline\_elevation});
    \item a combined well dashboard (\texttt{plot\_well\_dashboard}).
\end{itemize}

These utilities are separate from the numerical core, which keeps the simulator
structure clean and avoids mixing numerical algorithms with presentation logic.

\subsection*{\texttt{main\_wells.py}}

The main script acts as the execution layer of the simulator. Its role in the
present architecture is to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item create fluid objects;
    \item read survey files;
    \item instantiate wells, flowlines, chokes, and boundary nodes;
    \item assemble them into a \texttt{ProductionNetwork};
    \item call the solver;
    \item request plots and organize the final outputs.
\end{itemize}

Thus, \texttt{main\_wells.py} is the entry point that connects the modules into
a complete simulation workflow. Figure~\ref{fig:code-architecture} shows how the
modules interact during a typical simulation.

\subsection*{Summary of the Workflow}

The full workflow can be summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Survey data are read and converted into a usable trajectory
          representation \cite{Wolff1981}.
    \item Fluid properties are evaluated from the black-oil model
          \cite{Standing1977}.
    \item Hydraulic correlations are selected according to inclination and flow
          conditions \cite{BeggsBrill1973,HagedornBrown1965}.
    \item Physical components such as wells, flowlines, chokes, and nodes are
          instantiated.
    \item The network solver applies the required boundary conditions and
          computes the rates and pressures across the system.
    \item The results are visualized through nodal plots, well profiles,
          flowline traverses, and network summary figures.
\end{enumerate}

This modular architecture allows each part of the simulator to be validated
independently while preserving a clear integration path for full-field or
multiwell network calculations.

\subsection{PIPESIM Mechanistic versus Python Comparison}
\label{subsec:pipesim_vs_python}

\subsubsection{Overview and Code Structure}

To supplement the \emph{PIPESIM} \cite{Schlumberger2023} network simulations,
a Python model  was developed to replicate the wellbore
pressure traverses independently of a commercial simulator. The Python code
constructs a seven-well network identical in topology to the \emph{PIPESIM}
model: each well is represented as a constant-mass source at the sandface, and
the wellbore is discretised into 60 depth increments from perforations
(TVD~=~4{,}642~ft) to the wellhead. Boundary conditions (flowing bottomhole
pressure $P_{wf} = 2{,}710.4$~psia, $q_{\text{liq}} \approx 6{,}000$~STB/d
per well) were taken directly from the calibrated \emph{PIPESIM} results.

Two pressure-traverse functions were implemented:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Isothermal traverse:} assumes a constant mixture density
          ($\rho_{\text{mix}} = 48$~lb/ft$^{3}$) and a fixed Darcy-Weisbach
          friction factor ($f = 0.018$) \cite{Moody1944}, yielding a uniform
          pressure gradient throughout the tubing string. This is the primary
          function used for comparison with \emph{PIPESIM}.

    \item \textbf{Geothermal traverse:} introduces a linear temperature
          gradient from surface (80$^\circ$F) to bottomhole (200$^\circ$F,
          with sensitivity cases at 180$^\circ$F and 220$^\circ$F). Fluid
          density is adjusted at each depth increment using simplified
          temperature-dependent correlations for oil, water, and gas
          \cite{Standing1977}, providing a first-order estimate of the thermal
          effect on the pressure profile.
\end{enumerate}

For vertical tubing, the TUFFP Unified model \cite{Gomez2000} (M1) and the
Gomez Enhanced correlation with Xiao Modified Film transition \cite{Xiao1990}
(M2) were selected as the two mechanistic models for the \emph{PIPESIM}
simulations, both of which are well-established for deepwater, high-rate
multiphase applications. The Python implementation uses a simplified isothermal
gradient as an independent analytical baseline; the impact of this thermal
assumption on the pressure profile is discussed in the subsection below.

\subsubsection{Pressure Traverse Profile Comparison}

Figure~\ref{fig:pressure_traverse} presents the wellbore pressure traverse
profiles for all seven wells under three models: the Python isothermal model,
\emph{PIPESIM} Model~1 (M1, TUFFP Unified \cite{Gomez2000}), and \emph{PIPESIM}
Model~2 (M2, Gomez Enhanced / Xiao Modified Film \cite{Xiao1990}). The
\emph{PIPESIM} profiles are represented as straight lines connecting the known
sandface and wellhead pressures, since only endpoint values were extracted from
the simulator.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figs/pipesim_01_pressure_traverse.png}
    \caption{Wellbore pressure traverse profiles for all seven wells. Blue:
             Python isothermal model; red dashed: \emph{PIPESIM} M1 (TUFFP
             Unified \cite{Gomez2000}); green dash-dot: \emph{PIPESIM} M2
             (Gomez Enhanced / Xiao Modified Film \cite{Xiao1990}). All wells
             share TVD~=~4{,}642~ft and $P_{wf}=2{,}710.4$~psia.}
    \label{fig:pressure_traverse}
\end{figure}

The Python isothermal traverse predicts a wellhead pressure of 1{,}190~psia
uniformly across all seven wells, since the model uses a single constant mixture
density independent of flow rate. The two \emph{PIPESIM} mechanistic models yield
higher wellhead pressures in the range 1{,}282--1{,}346~psia, reflecting the more
rigorous treatment of multiphase flow regimes, slip between phases, and frictional
losses in the tubing string \cite{Gomez2000,Xiao1990}. The offset between the
Python and \emph{PIPESIM} curves is therefore a direct consequence of the
isothermal, single-density assumption rather than a network-level discrepancy.

\subsubsection{Wellhead Pressure and Tubing Pressure Drop}

Figures~\ref{fig:wellhead_pressure} and~\ref{fig:tubing_dp} quantify the
model-to-model spread at the wellhead and across the tubing string, respectively.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figs/pipesim_03_tubing_dp.png}

The Python model predicts a constant tubing $\Delta P$ of 1{,}520~psia across

of the pressure gradient by assuming a heavier-than-actual mixture throughout the

\subsubsection{Effect of Temperature Assumption on the Pressure Profile}
\label{subsubsec:temperature_effect}

Figure~\ref{fig:temperature_effect} isolates the effect of the temperature
assumption on the pressure traverse for representative well W1, comparing four
temperature profiles against the \emph{PIPESIM} M1 and M2 wellhead pressures.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figs/pipesim_06_temperature_effect.png}
    \caption{Effect of temperature assumption on the W1 wellbore pressure
             profile. Four Python cases are shown: isothermal at 200$^\circ$F
             (blue, solid); geothermal gradient 80--200$^\circ$F (red, dashed);
             shallow reservoir 80--180$^\circ$F (green, dash-dot); hot reservoir
             80--220$^\circ$F (orange, dotted). Vertical dashed lines mark the
             \emph{PIPESIM} M1 and M2 wellhead pressures.}
    \label{fig:temperature_effect}
\end{figure}

All four temperature cases produce nearly identical pressure profiles, with the
surface pressure clustering within a narrow band ($\approx$1{,}620--1{,}640~psia
at the wellhead in the Python geothermal cases). This insensitivity to temperature
arises because the simplified density correlations used in the geothermal traverse
produce only small changes in mixture density over the range of temperatures
considered \cite{Standing1977}. The dominant driver of the offset between the
Python profiles and the \emph{PIPESIM} endpoint values (1{,}292 and 1{,}304~psia)
is therefore the choice of multiphase flow correlation, specifically the absence
of a slip-corrected holdup model such as Hagedorn and Brown \cite{HagedornBrown1965},
rather than the thermal assumption.

These results confirm that for this well geometry and flow rate, adopting a
geothermal gradient in place of an isothermal assumption has a second-order effect
on wellhead pressure prediction, while the selection of the multiphase flow model
has a first-order effect.

\subsubsection{PIPESIM Flow Model Sensitivity: TUFFP Unified vs.\
               Gomez Enhanced / Xiao Modified Film}

Figures~\ref{fig:liquid_rates} and~\ref{fig:junction_pressures} present the
\emph{PIPESIM} model sensitivity study across the two mechanistic models for well
liquid rates and manifold junction pressures.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figs/pipesim_04_liquid_rates.png}
    \caption{Per-well total liquid rates from \emph{PIPESIM} M1 (TUFFP Unified
             \cite{Gomez2000}, red) and M2 (Gomez Enhanced / Xiao Modified Film
             \cite{Xiao1990}, green). Annotations show the M1--M2 difference in
             STB/d.}
    \label{fig:liquid_rates}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figs/pipesim_05_junction_pressures.png}
    \caption{Manifold junction pressures at nodes M1, M2, and M3 for
             \emph{PIPESIM} M1 and M2. Both models yield identical junction
             pressures (1{,}176.3, 1{,}140.6, and 1{,}182.5~psia respectively),
             indicating that the flow model choice does not propagate differences
             to the network junction level under these conditions.}
    \label{fig:junction_pressures}
\end{figure}

The two mechanistic models produce total network liquid rates that agree to within
61~STB/d out of approximately 45{,}000~STB/d (less than 0.14\% relative
difference). Well-level rate differences are small and non-systematic (ranging
from $-17$ to $+19$~STB/d), suggesting that the network operating point is stable
regardless of which correlation set is used.

At the wellhead, however, M2 (Gomez Enhanced / Xiao Modified Film \cite{Xiao1990})
consistently predicts higher pressures than M1 (TUFFP Unified \cite{Gomez2000})
by 13--36~psia. This systematic offset reflects the different slip and friction
treatments in the two mechanistic models: the Xiao Modified Film model for
stratified-annular transition tends to predict higher frictional pressure losses
in the vertical tubing compared to the TUFFP Unified approach
\cite{Xiao1990,Gomez2000}. In a facility design context, this spread defines the
model uncertainty band that should be carried forward into equipment-sizing
calculations.

The identical junction pressures produced by M1 and M2
(Figure~\ref{fig:junction_pressures}) indicate that the flowline and riser
hydraulics, which dominate the network pressure balance at the manifold level,
are insensitive to the choice of tubing correlation under these operating
conditions. Practical consequences of model selection are therefore most
significant for wellhead equipment ratings and choke sizing rather than for
pipeline or riser design.
